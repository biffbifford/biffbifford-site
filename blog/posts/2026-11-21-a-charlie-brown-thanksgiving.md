Every November, somebody asks the same question: what do you do when the guest list is bigger than the menu? In 1973, Charlie Brown found out the hard way.

*A Charlie Brown Thanksgiving* aired on CBS on Tuesday, November 20, 1973, two days before the holiday. It ran about 25 minutes. It was the third holiday special for the Peanuts gang, after *A Charlie Brown Christmas* in 1965 and *It's the Great Pumpkin, Charlie Brown* in 1966.

## The invitation nobody planned

The story starts with a phone call. Peppermint Patty, whose father is away for the holiday, invites herself to Charlie Brown's Thanksgiving dinner. She brings Marcie and Franklin along too.

There is just one problem. Charlie Brown isn't hosting a big dinner, and he can cook very little. At one point it sounds like "maybe toast" is all he can manage.

## What was on the table

Linus has an idea. If Peppermint Patty wants a Thanksgiving, her friends will give her one before the family dinner. Snoopy and Woodstock help, and Snoopy sets up a ping-pong table with chairs in the yard.

The menu is the famous part: toast, pan-fried popcorn, pretzel sticks, jelly beans and sundaes. Linus says a grace based on the story of the first Thanksgiving, and Snoopy deals out the plates like a casino dealer.

Peppermint Patty is not impressed. She stands up and complains that this isn't a real Thanksgiving dinner. Marcie reminds her, gently, that she invited herself. It is one of the most honest moments in the series, and it still gets a laugh.

## The apology

Peppermint Patty asks Marcie to apologize for her. Then she thinks better of it and apologizes herself. Charlie Brown calls his grandmother, who invites everyone to her house for dinner. They sing "Over the River and Through the Wood" on the way, and Charlie Brown notes that his own home is a condominium, not a grandmother's house.

At the end, Snoopy and Woodstock sit down to a traditional meal of their own.

## The seat people talk about

Franklin, the character who joined the strip in 1968, sits alone on one side of the ping-pong table. Many viewers today notice it and find it awkward.

The NPR piece on the special's 50th anniversary explains where it came from. Charles Schulz added Franklin after the assassination of Martin Luther King Jr., at a schoolteacher's suggestion. Robin Reed, who voiced Franklin at age 11, has said that at the time the scene "represented progress." Jean Schulz wrote in 2019 that there was no ulterior motive, and that the animator who drew the scene can't be identified now.

You can read it as a product of its time or as a thing the show would do differently today. Either way, it has kept people talking for decades.

## How it spread

The special was directed by Bill Melendez, who also voiced Snoopy and Woodstock, and Phil Roman. It won an Emmy in 1974. In the weekly ratings, it finished third, behind *All in the Family* and *Sanford & Son*.

CBS aired it every year through 1989, skipping only 1982, 1983 and 1988. ABC took it over in 2001. Since 2020 it has streamed on Apple TV+, so check there for this year's showing.

## Why it lasts

Everyone has had the Thanksgiving where things didn't go to plan. The oven failed, the guests showed up early, the turkey wasn't ready. This special says that a table is still a table, and that the people matter more than the menu.

So if your dinner this year comes together with toast and jelly beans, you're in good company.
