The King Goes to War: Clark Gable After Carole Lombard

By Biff Bifford · November 23, 2026

Clark Gable could have sat out the war. He was one of the biggest stars in the world, and he was too old for the draft. Instead, he spent part of 1943 in a B-17 over Europe, firing a machine gun at the enemy.

This is the written companion to the new BiffBifford documentary, The King Goes to War.

The loss that changed him

In January 1942, Carole Lombard was killed in the crash of an airliner. She was returning from a war bond tour, selling bonds to help pay for the fight. Gable was shattered.

Soon after, by one account, he sent a telegram to President Roosevelt asking how he could serve. The reply he reportedly got was short: "Stay where you are."

Joining up at 41

Gable did not stay where he was. On August 12, 1942, he enlisted in Los Angeles. He went through the 13-week Officer Candidate School and trained as an aerial gunner and photographer. The Army Air Forces had a plan for him: make a film that would recruit gunners, and put a star behind the camera and in the plane.

England and the B-17

By 1943 he was in England with the 351st Bomb Group at Polebrook, part of the Eighth Air Force. He went as a captain, and he flew with the crews in B-17s while his film team filmed the missions.

The official record shows five combat missions:

  • May 4, 1943: Antwerp, Belgium
  • July 10, 1943: Villacoublay, France
  • July 24, 1943: Heroya, Norway
  • August 12, 1943: a raid on Germany
  • September 23, 1943: Nantes, France

Some veterans remember him flying more. The records say five. Sources also disagree on a few details of where he flew and with whom, so treat the exact route of any one mission with caution.

Flak was a real danger on these raids. On one of them the plane was damaged by anti-aircraft fire. On another, the B-17 was shot up by German fighters and lost an engine, and the crew bailed out over England when fog closed in.

The reward story

You have probably heard that a top Nazi offered a reward for Gable's capture. The story is told as lore, and the details change depending on who tells it. It makes a good story. It is not something we can prove.

The film

Gable came home in November 1943 with about 50,000 feet of 16mm color film. It became Combat America, a 63-minute documentary about the men who flew. It was overshadowed that year by William Wyler's Memphis Belle, which came out at the same time.

Coming home

He was promoted to major and, at his own request, was relieved of active duty on June 12, 1944, because he was too old for combat flying. He received the Air Medal, and later the Distinguished Flying Cross.

He went back to Hollywood, but the men who served with him remembered something else. One sergeant called him "a great friend of the enlisted men as well as a great all-around guy."
